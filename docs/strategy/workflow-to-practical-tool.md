# Turning a difficult workflow into a practical tool

Status: Working exploration for the KERRCO offer and future website copy.
Date: November 22, 2026.
Related: [Local consulting strategy](local-ai-consulting.md) and [website direction](../website-rework-plan.md).

## The idea

> I help small businesses turn a difficult workflow into a practical tool. That might mean custom software, a useful automation, or a focused application of AI.

The useful next question is: **what would that actually look like for a business owner?**

A starting point could be: “Show me one job, from the first customer request to the point where the work is finished. Where does it get difficult?”

The opportunity might be a request that gets lost, information copied between systems, a schedule that only one person understands, or paperwork that has to be assembled by hand. The project starts with that specific friction and a clear idea of what would make the work easier.

This document explores a possible service process and how to explain it on the site. The examples are hypothetical; they are not completed client projects or promises of results. Pricing, schedules, and support terms remain to be decided.

## A concrete example: customer request to estimate

Imagine a small contractor receiving work requests through phone calls, emails, and text messages. One person writes down the details, asks for missing information, arranges a site visit, and eventually prepares an estimate.

### The current workflow

1. A customer calls or sends a message.
2. Someone records the request in a notebook, message thread, or spreadsheet.
3. The owner asks for details that were missed: address, photos, scope, or availability.
4. A site visit is arranged through more calls or messages.
5. Notes from the visit are used to prepare an estimate.
6. Someone remembers to follow up, or the request stalls.

The friction is at the handoffs: knowing what information is missing, who owns the next step, and whether anything has been forgotten.

### A possible first tool

Start with **request intake and tracking**, ending when the request is ready for an estimate. Keep estimating, invoicing, and the rest of the business outside the first version.

| Step | What the tool could do | What the person still does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Capture the request | Provide a short intake form and a way for staff to enter phone requests | Confirm what the customer needs |
| Collect details | Flag missing contact details, job location, or photos | Ask follow-up questions and handle unusual jobs |
| Track progress | Show new requests, requests needing information, site visits, and requests ready for an estimate | Assign responsibility and update progress |
| Prepare the handoff | Put the request, photos, and visit notes in one place | Decide scope, pricing, and whether to take the work |
| Follow up | Remind staff when a request needs attention | Review and send customer communication |

The customer can still call. Staff have a common place to put the request and see what happens next.

### Where each kind of technology could fit

- **Existing tools:** The current software may already support the intake form or shared tracking view. Configuration and a clearer process could be enough.
- **Automation:** A form could create a request and notify the responsible person. A rule could flag requests without a next action.
- **Custom software:** A focused application could connect the information and statuses when existing tools cannot support the workflow well.
- **AI:** If reading long messages is a real bottleneck, AI could draft a request summary for staff to check against the original. Missing details should stay missing rather than being guessed.

These are possible approaches, not four required parts of every project. AI would need to earn its place through a useful, reviewable result. In this example, a form and a shared work list may provide the main improvement.

### What would count as useful?

Before building, agree on a few things to observe during a pilot:

- Can staff find every pilot request and identify its next action?
- Is the information needed for an estimate easier to find?
- How much staff time goes into collecting and re-entering details?
- Does the person covering the office understand the request without asking the owner?

Use actual observations to compare the old and new process. Record what improved, what still needs work, and any new effort the tool creates. There is no savings claim until there is evidence.

## What working together could look like

### 1. Start with a conversation

The business owner describes the work, who does it, and where it causes trouble. They do not need a software specification or an idea for an app.

Useful questions:

- Can you walk me through the last time this happened?
- What starts the work, and how do you know it is finished?
- Who touches it, and where is information handed off?
- How often does it happen?
- What happens when something is missed or entered incorrectly?
- What have you already tried?

**Output:** A shared description of one problem and a practical next step. The site already offers a free initial conversation; detailed investigation or implementation would have a separate scope.

### 2. Walk through the real process

Look at a representative job with the people who do the work. Follow the forms, messages, spreadsheets, and existing software. Include an awkward case, such as missing information or a changed appointment.

Use sample or redacted material where possible. Agree on access before looking at customer records or connecting accounts.

Write down:

- The trigger, steps, people, and finish point.
- Where each piece of information lives.
- Repeated entry, delays, and places where work gets lost.
- Exceptions and decisions that require a person.
- Practical constraints, such as phone use in the field or unreliable connectivity.
- A baseline for the improvement being sought.

**Output:** A simple workflow map and a recommendation. That might be a process change, better use of existing software, or a proposed build.

### 3. Agree on the smallest useful project

Choose a first version that completes one useful piece of work. For the contractor example, that could be capturing a request and getting it ready for an estimate.

Define the users, included steps, required information, integrations, and acceptance criteria. Also define the price, schedule, client responsibilities, and what happens if the scope changes. Investigate uncertain integrations before promising them.

Decide who owns the application and accounts, who pays recurring costs, where business data is stored, and how it can be exported. Agree on what happens after delivery, including support and changes.

**Output:** A written scope the owner can evaluate before committing to implementation.

### 4. Make the idea tangible

Show a small prototype using sample information. It might be a few screens, a form and work list, or a demonstration of one automation.

Have the people who will use it walk through an ordinary job and an exception. Find out whether it fits their day: whether the form asks sensible questions, the status names are clear, and the next action is obvious.

**Output:** A reviewed example of the intended workflow, with changes identified before the full build. A prototype may still use manual steps or simulated connections; make those limits clear.

### 5. Build and try it with real work

Build the agreed scope, then run a limited pilot with agreed users and records. Decide which system holds the official record during the pilot so jobs do not disappear between two processes.

Check the full path, including missing information, duplicate submissions, failed connections, and changes made by staff. If AI is included, check incorrect and unsupported outputs and how staff catch them. Establish a manual fallback for work that cannot wait.

**Output:** Evidence that the tool handles the agreed work, plus a clear list of remaining issues before broader use.

### 6. Hand it over and review the result

Help staff use the tool in their normal work. Provide short instructions, access ownership, data export and recovery information, and the agreed support arrangements.

Compare the pilot observations with the baseline. Fix problems within the agreed scope and decide together whether another improvement is worth pursuing.

**Output:** A tool people can use, a clear owner, and an evidence-based decision about what comes next.

## Other workflows worth exploring

These are conversation starters, not established service packages.

| Starting problem | Possible focused tool | What needs to be understood first |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Job notes and photos are scattered across messages | A job record that collects field notes and prepares a report draft | Who records the work and who approves the final report |
| Orders are copied between a form and an office spreadsheet | Validated order capture with a shared status view | Which record is authoritative and how changes are handled |
| Staff repeatedly search manuals and procedures | Document search that shows the supporting source | Which documents are current and who may see them |
| A weekly report takes hours to assemble | A repeatable import and report with missing-data checks | Whether the source information is consistent and complete |

The common pattern is a specific task with a clear beginning, a useful output, and someone responsible for checking it.

## How this could become website content

The service page already introduces custom software, automation, and practical AI. A useful addition would make the process easier to picture:

1. Keep the current positioning near the top.
2. Add one short, explicitly hypothetical example showing a workflow before and after.
3. Explain the project process in four simple steps.
4. Link to the existing software portfolio for completed project evidence.
5. End with a consultation invitation that asks about the work causing trouble.

Keep the detailed delivery process in this document. Visitors mainly need to understand the kind of problem they can bring, how the work would begin, and what a useful result might look like.

### Draft section: what this might look like

**From scattered requests to a clear next step.**

Imagine customer requests coming through calls, texts, and emails. Details are missing, notes are hard to find, and follow-up depends on someone remembering.

A focused tool could give staff one place to record each request, collect the information they need, and see what happens next. We would start with that part of the work and try it with the people who handle it every day.

*An example of a possible project. The right approach depends on your workflow and existing tools.*

### Draft section: how we get there

**Start with the work. Build something useful.**

1. **Show me where it gets difficult.** We walk through the task, the people involved, and where time or information gets lost.
2. **Choose a practical first step.** We consider your existing tools and agree on a focused improvement, scope, and cost.
3. **Try it in your workflow.** You and the people doing the work help shape the tool and test it with real tasks.
4. **Put it to use.** I help you get started, hand over the agreed documentation, and review how it is working.

### Draft invitation

**What is one task you wish worked better?**

Tell me what happens today, where it gets frustrating, and what would make it easier. You do not need to know what software you need. The initial conversation is free; further work is scoped separately.

Button: **Tell me about your workflow**

Destination: The existing consultation section on the contact page.

## Decisions to develop next

- **Lead example:** Is contractor intake the best illustration, or would a different workflow better reflect the work KERRCO wants to attract?
- **First engagement:** Should workflow mapping be offered as a separately priced deliverable before a build, or included in a small project when the scope is already clear?
- **Delivery model:** Which projects should be handed over to client-owned hosting and accounts, and when would ongoing KERRCO hosting make sense?
- **Support:** What help is included after delivery, and what would require an ongoing agreement?
- **Evidence:** Which existing project can demonstrate a similar workflow with confirmed facts and permission to share supporting material?

A sensible first website addition would be the short example and four-step process above. Finalize the service terms before turning them into public promises.
